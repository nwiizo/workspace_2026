# CFP Authoring Guidelines

## Scope

Apply these rules when drafting, revising, or reviewing CFPs and conference proposals in this directory. Use the repository's `cfp-review` skill for both submission-ready drafts and staged reviews.

## Establish the Event Context First

- Record the event name, intended audience, theme, talk duration, categories, audience level, title and abstract limits, required fields, selection criteria, and important keywords.
- When current selection criteria, accepted talks, or industry trends affect the proposal, verify them from official event sources. Review the previous two or three editions when available.
- Distinguish confirmed facts from inference. Never invent event requirements, acceptance trends, implementation experience, metrics, failures, or outcomes.
- If noncritical information is missing, draft with explicit assumptions. Ask only when a missing choice would materially change the proposal or make it invalid.

## Design the Proposal Before Writing It

Define the proposal in one sentence:

> For [audience] facing [problem], this talk uses [experience, evidence, or method] to teach [distinctive insight], so they can [apply it at work].

Then confirm all of the following:

- One primary audience and one central problem
- One clear claim or distinctive insight
- Evidence appropriate to the talk type: firsthand experience, a concrete example, verified data, a failure, or an explicit conceptual method
- A credible reason to hear the talk now
- Concrete takeaways that fit the available talk time

Treat `summary -> background -> evaluation -> proposal -> future implications` as a thinking order, not as mandatory abstract headings. Keep the scope narrow enough to deliver what the abstract promises.

## Write for the First Screening

- Make the title accurately communicate the subject and distinctive angle. Respect the event's exact character limit.
- Make the first two or three lines understandable without reading the rest. State the problem or claim, the talk's approach, and the audience benefit or urgency.
- Use trend terms only when the talk contains a substantive, specific connection. Do not keyword-stuff or disguise a generic talk with fashionable vocabulary.
- Prefer concrete nouns, actions, examples, trade-offs, and verified numbers over broad claims. If evidence is unavailable, frame the talk honestly as a blueprint, analysis, or hypothesis.

## Make Audience Value Explicit

Ensure the abstract answers these questions:

1. What will attendees learn?
2. How can they apply it to their work?
3. Why should they hear it now?

Name prerequisites or the expected audience level when needed. Explain specialist terminology that is not shared by that audience.

## Keep the Abstract Scannable

- Use short paragraphs, usually three or four sentences or fewer.
- Prefer concise sentences. Treat roughly 40 Japanese characters as a readability heuristic, not a hard limit.
- Keep each paragraph focused on one role: problem, approach/content, evidence, or takeaway.
- Use headings, lists, and emphasis only when the submission form preserves that formatting. Do not rely on styling to make the logic understandable.
- Remove repeated claims, throat-clearing, excessive background, and details that do not affect the acceptance decision.

## Review Gates

Before presenting a CFP as ready to submit, verify:

- The title and opening pass a five-second comprehension test.
- The event fit is explicit without forcing the theme.
- The title, abstract, category, level, keywords, and takeaways describe the same talk.
- Claims, numbers, trends, and case studies are supportable.
- Successes do not hide relevant constraints, failures, or trade-offs.
- The promised content is feasible within the talk duration.
- Every submission field and character or keyword limit passes a mechanical check.
- A reviewer unfamiliar with the draft can identify the audience, problem, insight, evidence, and takeaway.

Prioritize content value over stylistic polish. Give actionable feedback with replacement text or a specific addition; never stop at comments such as "make it clearer" or "add more detail."
